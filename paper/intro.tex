\subsection{Background}

Large language models are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that
interact with environments, tools, and other agents.  Recent work has shown
that LLM-powered agents can exhibit emergent social behaviours in open-ended
simulation~\cite{park2023generative}, coordinate within multi-agent
frameworks~\cite{wu2023autogen}, and interleave reasoning with
action~\cite{yao2023react}.  A natural extension of this research agenda is
to ask whether LLM agents can act as economically meaningful negotiators ---
that is, whether they produce bargaining behaviour that is interpretable
through the lens of established economic theory.

Bilateral bargaining is among the best-characterised settings in economic
theory.  Rubinstein's alternating-offer model~\cite{rubinstein1982} predicts
that patient agents extract larger surplus shares through time-preference
discounting.  The anchoring literature~\cite{tversky1974} establishes anchoring as a
general cognitive heuristic; in negotiation contexts, Galinsky and
Mussweiler~\cite{galinsky2001} show that first offers systematically bias
final agreements.  Deadline pressure effects have been documented in human
experiments~\cite{roth1988deadline}, and decentralised bilateral markets are
expected to produce equilibrium prices with persistent price
dispersion~\cite{rubinstein1985}.  These theoretical predictions provide
clear, testable benchmarks for evaluating agent behaviour.

Despite growing interest in LLM negotiation, existing studies typically
focus on multi-issue dialogue games~\cite{lewis2017dealornodeal,
abdelnabi2024llmdeliberation}, personality-driven
interaction~\cite{mao2024personalities}, or assistive human--AI
bargaining~\cite{chawla2023assistive}.  These designs evaluate negotiation
capability but do not systematically test LLM agents against the specific
quantitative predictions of classical bargaining theory.  Furthermore, most
studies operate at a single level of analysis without connecting
micro-level bargaining outcomes to macro-level market dynamics within a
single controlled platform.  The practical significance of this gap is
growing: automated negotiation is entering consumer and enterprise
markets~\cite{davidson2024automatedrisky}, and understanding where LLM
behaviour aligns with or departs from economic theory is an important input
for anticipating the consequences of deployment.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

The central question motivating this thesis is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can LLM-based autonomous agents serve as economically meaningful
negotiators in a language-mediated buyer--seller market simulator, and under
what simulator and protocol designs do meaningful micro- and macro-level
patterns emerge?}
\end{quote}

This question cannot be answered by evaluating LLM outputs in isolation.
Simulator design choices --- the feasibility gate that enforces settlement,
the prompt architecture that structures agent reasoning, and the protocol
design that governs turn-taking and termination --- all materially alter the
observable behaviour of agents.  A feasibility gate that deterministically
accepts profitable offers, for example, may suppress the very dynamics (late
concessions, deadline pressure) that an experiment is intended to measure.
These design choices are therefore first-order experimental variables, not
neutral background conditions.

A second challenge is attribution.  Any observed outcome in an LLM
simulation reflects the combined system: model, prompt, gate, and protocol.
Disentangling the contribution of LLM decision-making from the contribution
of simulator infrastructure requires careful logging, explicit ablation
studies, and conservative interpretation.  This thesis builds a platform
designed to support such analysis, though it does not fully resolve the
attribution problem within the scope of a single project.

\subsection{Research Objectives}

This thesis pursues four objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Design and implement a configurable multi-agent negotiation
    simulator that enforces hard economic constraints, supports multiple
    agent types, and provides structured event logging sufficient to
    distinguish gate-driven from LLM-driven settlements.

    \item Conduct a systematic evaluation across seven experiments covering
    concession dynamics, anchoring bias, deadline pressure, market price
    discovery, shock response, mechanism design, and supply--demand
    responsiveness.

    \item Characterise, with appropriate conservatism, where platform
    outcomes are directionally consistent with classical theory and where
    they depart from it, distinguishing genuine LLM behaviour from
    gate-induced artefacts wherever the evidence permits.

    \item Analyse how simulator design choices --- including the feasibility
    gate, prompt structure, and matching mechanism --- function as
    experimental variables that shape the apparent behaviour of agents.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}

This thesis makes three contributions.  All results characterise the
combined system (LLM~+~rethink~+~gate~+~prompt) under specific conditions; they
should not be generalised across model families or scales without further
ablation and replication.

\textbf{Simulator.}  We present a configurable multi-agent negotiation
platform for buyer--seller bilateral bargaining.  The platform enforces
private constraints (buyer value, seller cost, budgets), implements an
alternating-offers protocol with configurable round horizons, and applies a
deterministic feasibility gate that accepts clearly profitable offers without
calling the LLM.  LLM outputs pass through an internal rethink loop that
corrects parse and self-constraint errors before they reach the session's
gate and ActionJudge.  Every gate intervention is logged so that gate-driven and
LLM-driven settlements can be distinguished in analysis.  A market-level
execution layer connects individual negotiation sessions to aggregate
outcomes, enabling simultaneous analysis of micro-level bargaining dynamics
and macro-level price and liquidity statistics.  Three agent types are
supported: a deterministic rule-based baseline, an LLM reactive agent, and
an LLM deliberative agent whose structured reasoning draws on chain-of-thought
prompting~\cite{wei2022chain}.

\textbf{Experimental evaluation.}  We conduct seven experiments totalling
4,120 negotiation sessions using a single model (\texttt{llama3.2:3b} via
Ollama at temperature~0.2).  Experiments A--C (concession curves, anchoring,
deadline pressure) are replicated across three seeds; Experiments D and E
(market price discovery and stochastic shock response) are single-seed
exploratory runs whose results carry higher uncertainty;
Experiments H and I (mechanism design, supply--demand shifts) are replicated
across three seeds.  Several results are directionally consistent with
economic predictions: deal rates scale with zone-of-possible-agreement
(ZOPA) width (2.7\% at ZOPA\,=\,\$20 versus 100\% at ZOPA\,$\geq$\,\$40),
a pattern consistent with gate-enforced feasibility constraints as well as
agent behaviour.  Structural supply shocks are associated with
near-complete cost pass-through (+17.9\% price from a +\$20 cost shift
across three seeds), and surplus-maximising matching improves deal rates by
16~percentage points and allocative efficiency by 5.8~percentage points
relative to random matching.  Two prominent null results --- no anchoring
effect ($r = -0.148$ between first offer and final price) and no
deadline-driven concession acceleration --- are substantially confounded by
the feasibility gate and prompt design, and should not be interpreted as
evidence about LLM cognition in general.

\textbf{Methodological analysis.}  We provide an analysis of how simulator
design choices function as first-order experimental variables.  The
feasibility gate drives approximately 40\% of all acceptances globally and
effectively 100\% of acceptances in Experiments A--C, making it difficult to
attribute positive outcomes in those experiments to LLM decision-making
without gate ablation.  Prompt architecture also has measurable effects: the
deliberative agent produces seller counter-offers approximately \$7 lower
than the reactive agent on the first counter, a difference consistent with a prompting effect, though
alternative explanations cannot be excluded without controlled ablation.  These findings suggest that the
design of the simulation infrastructure is not separable from the findings it
produces --- a methodological concern that may extend to other efforts to use
LLM agents as proxies for human economic behaviour.

Multi-model comparison and gate ablation at the experiment level
are identified as priorities for future work.
