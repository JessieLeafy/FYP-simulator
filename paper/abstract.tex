\begin{abstract}
We present a configurable multi-agent negotiation simulator for evaluating LLM bargaining behaviour in alternating-offer bilateral negotiation under private economic constraints, and report three contributions: the simulator itself, a structured experimental evaluation, and a methodological analysis of how simulator design choices affect observed outcomes.

Using a single 3B-parameter language model (\texttt{llama3.2:3b}), we conduct seven experiments totalling 4,120 negotiation sessions across three levels of analysis: micro-level bargaining dynamics (concession curves, anchoring bias, deadline pressure), macro-level market outcomes (price discovery, stochastic shock response, structural supply-demand shifts), and institutional mechanism effects (matching algorithm comparison).

Several platform-level outcomes are directionally consistent with classical bargaining and market theory: deal rates scale with the width of the zone of possible agreement, the buyer-first protocol produces systematic surplus asymmetry, structural supply-demand shifts produce directionally correct price responses with near-complete cost pass-through on the supply side (+17.9\% from a +\$20 cost shift across three seeds), and surplus-maximising matching improves deal rates by 16 percentage points and allocative efficiency by 5.8 percentage points relative to random matching.  In single-seed exploratory runs, market prices show no systematic trend over 30~ticks and exogenous shocks reduce prices and liquidity in theoretically predicted directions.

However, a configurable feasibility gate---necessary to prevent small-model output failures---drives approximately 40\% of all acceptances (and effectively all acceptances in the micro-level experiments), meaning that many positive outcomes reflect gate enforcement and protocol structure rather than agent-level decision-making.  Agents do not exhibit two well-documented human bargaining heuristics---anchoring bias and deadline-driven concession acceleration---but these null results are heavily confounded by the gate (which settles deals before the relevant dynamics can manifest) and by prompt design choices, making it unclear whether the absences reflect LLM cognition or experimental design constraints.

A central methodological finding is that simulator design choices---the feasibility gate, prompt architecture, and protocol structure---function as first-order experimental variables that materially alter the apparent economic behaviour of LLM agents.  The platform makes these choices explicit, quantifies their involvement, and logs every gate-fired settlement separately, enabling assessment of how much observed behaviour is agent-driven versus mechanically enforced.  The empirical results characterise one model under one prompt architecture and should not be generalised without gate ablation, prompt ablation, and multi-model comparison.

\end{abstract}
